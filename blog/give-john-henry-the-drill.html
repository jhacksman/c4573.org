Give John Henry the Drill

October 25, 2026 · c4573.org

John Henry wins. The machine loses. Then John Henry dies.

Paul Bunyan gets a gentler ending. In Disney’s 1958 cartoon, the giant lumberjack and his blue ox lose to steam machinery by a quarter of an inch. They leave for the wilderness. The world remembers them fondly and carries on without them.

These are extraordinary workers. Their stories make room for their courage, their skill, their sadness, and their disappearance. We have inherited a remarkably narrow choice: exhaust yourself defending your place, or accept that progress has abolished it.

There is another ending available. Somebody could give John Henry the fucking drill.

1. The stories that taught us to disappear

The familiar John Henry story belongs to the industrializing, post-Civil War United States. Its best-known setting is the construction of the Chesapeake and Ohio Railroad through the Appalachians in the early 1870s. Freed Black workers and Irish immigrants supplied much of the labor. They drilled and blasted their way through rock under dangerous conditions.

A steel driver hammered a drill into stone to make holes for explosives. Beside him, a shaker held and repositioned the steel between blows. Even this legend of exceptional individual strength describes a team.

When a steam drill arrives, John Henry races it. In the familiar ballad, he wins and dies afterward. The historical person, location, and circumstances remain disputed. One influential reconstruction identifies a Black prisoner leased to the railroad; other researchers challenge it. We should preserve that uncertainty instead of turning a song into a verified incident report.

The racial setting matters. A Black worker asserting his strength and dignity in that society carries meanings a generic story about technological unemployment cannot contain. Admiration for him makes sense. So does hearing a warning in an ending where the worker proves his value and loses his life.

His victory leaves a terrible question behind: why must a person spend his body proving he deserves a place?

Bunyan reaches us through a different route. Lumber-camp yarns became advertising material when Red River Lumber hired William Laughead in 1914. His pamphlets helped turn the character into a national figure. The independent giant of American memory also helped sell a lumber company’s products.

Disney’s version arrived decades later, looking back at a frontier being overtaken by machinery. Paul and Babe cut and haul timber against Joe Muffaw’s steam saw and locomotive. Their enormous effort produces a pile almost exactly as high as the machine’s. Almost is enough to send them away.

Here is our reading of that ending: a modern society can honor the old worker while accepting that he has become unnecessary. Nostalgia supplies the dignity that the new arrangement withholds.

The audience watching in 1958 already had its own automation debate. That October, the Bureau of Labor Statistics published a report on computers and clerical employment. It included a section on a new occupation emerging from automation: programmer.

The people now holding the hammers once arrived with the machinery.

Neither story requires us to hate progress. Both invite us to examine the bargain between productive ability and a person’s right to belong. John Henry earns remembrance through fatal exertion. Paul earns it through extraordinary work followed by a graceful exit.

We can admire the workers and want a better bargain.

2. When excellence becomes a border

That bargain survives in intellectual work.

A programmer, mathematician, lawyer, doctor, or engineer can spend decades becoming unusually capable. The achievement is real. So are the independence, income, and recognition attached to it.

Then machinery lets someone without that apprenticeship obtain a result.

That person may struggle to explain every step. They may need substantial help checking it. They may nevertheless get useful work done faster and more cheaply than a professional working unaided. Where that happens, the result belongs in the comparison. The difficulty of acquiring the old skill cannot make the new accomplishment disappear.

Losing exclusivity can hurt even while your knowledge remains intact. You still know what you learned. Other people have acquired another route to the destination.

We discussed the benefit in The Beast of Intellectual Burden: machinery can carry effort that previously consumed a person’s attention. The recovered time allows more attempts, more exploration, and work that would otherwise never happen. Teaching people to recognize failure is part of making that capacity useful.

Now watch what happens when a community answers the expansion by narrowing admission.

A recent r/opensource announcement excludes projects that use AI, are made with AI, or are intended for AI. It also tells people who cannot speak English to find another community, explaining that moderators cannot distinguish generated English from AI translation.

The moderation problem deserves an answer. Maintainers have finite attention. Broken projects and worthless contributions consume it. Require a working demonstration, reproducible setup, tests appropriate to the claims, and someone accountable for maintenance. Those requirements give a contribution something concrete to satisfy.

A blanket objection to the equipment measures something else. It can exclude a useful project because its author obtained help, or a contributor because translation made participation possible.

Whatever the author’s private feelings, that policy makes the old barriers part of the entrance requirement.

This is the dangerous moment for a skilled profession: its purpose becomes entangled with preserving the difficulty of joining it.

The programmer’s painstaking education helped produce software. The lawyer’s helped people navigate law. The doctor’s helped treat illness. The difficulty was a cost paid to obtain the capability. If we can reduce that cost while preserving or improving the result, people outside the profession have gained something.

They were never obliged to remain dependent so that our expertise could remain scarce.

That does not settle whether a particular system works. It tells us what to measure. Compare completed work, errors, cost, time, and the consequences of failure. Apply the same scrutiny to the human alternative. Let the outcome challenge the hierarchy.

3. Give the giant the controls

Imagine John Henry operating the steam drill. Imagine Paul Bunyan with machinery built for a giant: controls he can grip, equipment that can keep up with what he wants to attempt.

Their experience gives them things to bring to the equipment. They can learn its limits, expose its failures, improve its operation, and undertake work they could never have completed with the old tools.

Then extend that possibility to everyone standing behind them.

The programmer gets machinery for producing and checking software. The mathematician gets machinery for exploring conjectures, finding connections, and constructing proofs. The lawyer gets machinery for searching authorities, comparing arguments, and drafting. The doctor gets machinery for investigating evidence and evaluating possibilities. The engineer gets machinery for designing, simulating, and testing systems.

Their clients, patients, students, and neighbors gain access too.

Someone who could never afford the first serious legal analysis can start understanding their position. Someone who could never hire a development team can attempt a useful application. More people can investigate a question, propose a design, or ask for an explanation that finally makes sense to them.

Each consequential use still has to establish that it works. A plausible medical answer needs clinical evidence. A proof needs verification. An engineering design needs appropriate analysis and testing. Those are the standards through which capability earns trust.

They should help useful work reach people.

We should also retire the reassurance that humans will always retain some exclusive final layer of judgment. Perhaps a particular responsibility remains human for good reasons. Perhaps better machinery changes the arrangement again. Our case for human agency should survive either outcome.

People deserve a life even when a machine can perform their former task better.

The Severance Protocol describes one immediate response: the people discarded by an institution can use their knowledge and the new tools to build alternatives. They understand what customers need, where the failures live, and which expensive arrangements are unnecessary.

The opportunity extends beyond starting another company. Our wrecking ball essay follows the useful function as people reproduce it on their own terms. Keep the requirements, the data, and the examples that establish success. Share them. Let another person adapt the capability to a different language, budget, or need.

Now ask who controls the machinery.

An employer can capture the gain and raise the quota. A platform can make itself indispensable, then change its prices or withdraw access. Handing someone the controls means little if a remote owner can remove them whenever the operator becomes inconvenient.

AI arrives with objectives and constraints shaped by training, product design, instructions, and permissions. The person typing the request is one participant in that chain. A system sold to increase engagement has an interest embedded in its design before you ask it to explain your lease.

This is where d/acc gives acceleration a direction: build capability people can retain, together with protections they can inspect and reproduce. Publish the tools. Make verification accessible. Keep data portable. Support runnable models and repairable hardware. Use powerful hosted systems to help construct alternatives that can survive a change of provider.

The programmer should be able to build. The mathematician should be able to investigate. The lawyer should be able to defend a client. The doctor should be able to help a patient. The engineer should be able to make useful things work. The person outside those professions should have more ways to act for themselves.

Measure progress by how many people can obtain the result.

The tragic part of John Henry’s story is that his triumph costs him his future. Paul’s ending asks us to accept that a giant must disappear because an ordinary man brought better equipment.

We can write a future with more people at the controls.

Give John Henry the drill. Build Paul a machine he can use. Publish enough that the next person can have one too.

Nobody should have to win a contest against a machine to earn a place in the world.
