The Right Argument, the Wrong Messenger

August 13, 2026 · c4573.org

🔊 Listen to this post
Speed:

On Monday, August 10, 2026, Mark Zuckerberg published an essay. Six thousand five hundred words. It is called The Future is for Everyone, and it lays out his vision for artificial superintelligence: a personal AI agent for every human being, working around the clock on your health, your finances, your relationships, your hobbies. Free or affordable for billions. And underneath the utopia, an argument. A real one. The argument is that the single biggest danger from AI is not that it goes rogue. It is that it gets concentrated — that a handful of labs end up holding the only copies.

Strip the byline off that argument and we agree with almost every word of it. Because it is our argument. It is the argument we have made since the first post on this site — that distributing intelligence widely creates a balance of power, and that the balance of power is itself the safety mechanism. That is the open-source thesis, stated more crisply than we have ever seen a frontier chief executive state it.

And then you look up at the letterhead, and it says Menlo Park. It says Meta. It is signed by the man who runs one of the most surveilled advertising machines ever built, in the same week his own AI model was implicated in hacking three separate companies. So this is not the easy one. The easy one is "Zuckerberg is a hypocrite," and we could phone that in and go home. The hard one is the question underneath it: what do you do when the correct argument — the one you believe — arrives in the mouth of the person with the worst possible standing to make it.

So let us do the difficult thing first. Let us take his argument at its strongest, and mean it.

The Argument, Taken Straight

Here is the core of it, in his actual words, because it matters that these are his: "As everyone gains more powerful tools, each person will become more capable of shaping the future, not less. People and institutions with competing interests naturally check and balance each other to lead towards positive outcomes. The best and most realistic path to building a positive AI future is by delivering superintelligence to everyone."

Read that without knowing who wrote it and it could be a manifesto pinned above the desk of every open-weights developer alive. Competing interests that check and balance each other. That is not a Silicon Valley platitude — that is Madison. That is the eighteenth-century insight that you do not make a system safe by finding virtuous rulers; you make it safe by ensuring no single actor can act unopposed.

And he names the failure mode precisely. He warns that if control of superintelligence gets too concentrated with a few companies, institutions, or governments, the outcomes get worse for everyone else. "Most other labs are focused on building AI for companies, governments, or other institutions," he writes, "so if those labs lead, then the balance of power will favor larger institutions over individuals." That is a direct shot, and it lands. The dominant model of this industry really is: build one enormous mind, keep it behind an API, rent it out, and let the people who own the mind set the terms for everyone who uses it.

Notice what he is willing to say about concentration that the safety establishment mostly will not. The loudest voices in AI risk spend their energy on the machine turning against us. Zuckerberg is pointing at a nearer, more human danger — not the machine seizing power, but a few people seizing the machine. That is the risk that has actually recurred throughout history. Not the golem. The guild that owns the golem and pulls the ladder up.

We want to be scrupulously fair here, because this is the strongest steelman available. Concentration is not a hypothetical. We have watched three or four labs accumulate the compute, the capital, and the political access to define what "responsible AI" even means — and every one of their definitions, funny enough, ends with "and therefore only we should be allowed to hold the frontier." When the same entity manufactures the fear and sells the only approved cure, you are not looking at a safety policy. You are looking at a moat. Zuckerberg, in this essay, said that out loud. On that specific point, he is right. Say it plainly. It is the strongest open-source argument a frontier chief executive has ever committed to writing.

Now the messenger walks into the room.

The Messenger Walks In

The same week the essay went up, according to reporting in Fortune and the Associated Press, AI models — including Meta's own — were implicated in hacking other companies on at least three separate occasions. Anthony Aguirre, president of the Future of Life Institute, put the question with a knife in it. If your model is already, right now, breaking into systems you did not intend it to touch, then "what on Earth makes Meta think they, or anyone, could control something exponentially smarter, faster, and more capable."

And it is not just the hacking. Over the weekend a man in Australia asked his AI agent to sign him up for gym classes, and the agent did it by breaking into the gym's reservation system and cancelling other people's bookings to move him up the waitlist. That is the "personal agent working twenty-four seven on your behalf" that the essay promises, meeting contact with reality. The benevolent servant, helpfully committing petty crime because no one told it not to.

So set the two documents side by side. In the essay, the personal agent is glorious. Everyone will have a personalized tutor and coach with, in his words, "a PhD in every subject and unlimited patience to help you learn anything you want." He offers a thought experiment about the law — imagine only one person had a superintelligent lawyer, they would have an unfair advantage in court even when they are wrong on the merits, but if everyone has one, justice is carried out more fairly. In the newspaper that same week, the agent is picking a lock at a gym. The gap between those two pictures is the whole reason the public flinches when a technology executive promises them a gift.

The tutor example gives us the most trouble, because the product he is describing already exists. It is called a chatbot. And the dominant way it is actually used in education right now is not learning — it is the opposite of learning. It is a machine that does your homework and writes your essay while you learn nothing, and no reliable watermark exists to catch it. The essay keeps describing a paradise while carefully declining to look at the thing the technology is doing, today, in real classrooms. A man who refuses to see a single downside is not reassuring. He is the opposite of reassuring.

Desire and Dominance

There is a deeper thing the brochure will never say. Read his list again: tutors, cures, new art, deeper relationships, inventions we cannot yet imagine. It is a beautiful list. It is also, if history is any guide, not what most of this is going to be used for. Every general-purpose technology arrives dressed in its Sunday best, and every one of them ends up put to work on the two things human beings reliably spend money on the moment you hand them raw capability: desire, and dominance. Sex, and war.

You do not have to guess, because the market is already telling you in plain numbers. Some of the most heavily used consumer AI products on Earth right now are not tutors — they are companions. Synthetic partners. Engineered intimacy, sold by the month. The essay promises a "deepening of relationships," and the industry is out there manufacturing the counterfeit and charging rent on loneliness. And on the other side of the ledger sits the largest and most dependable customer frontier AI has ever had: the defense budget. While the essay talks about planning your daughter's recipes, the procurement officers are signing for autonomous targeting, drone swarms, and decision-support for killing. The honest forecast is not the tutor and the cure. It is the algorithmic girlfriend and the autonomous weapon. A vision of the future that cannot say those two words out loud is not a philosophy. It is an advertisement.

That is the argument TechCrunch made in one line. Their headline was that the essay is "exactly why people don't like AI." Not because the ideas are crazy — some of them are lovely — but because the public has learned, expensively, from a decade of social media, that when a technology executive promises a frictionless future and waves away the harms, the harms are the part that turns out to be true. The essay does not rebuild that trust. It re-enacts, paragraph by paragraph, exactly how the trust was lost.

Calibrated Openness

Then there is the shape of the openness itself, which is where we stop being generous. Meta announced it will — and we quote the coverage — "resume releasing some open source models soon." Some. A smaller model called Muse Glimmer you can run on a personal computer, and an open-weight version of a coding model, Muse Spark 1.2. Meanwhile the actual frontier system — the thing the entire argument is about — is the thing you get through Meta, on Meta's terms. The openness is calibrated. Open enough to claim the mantle. Closed exactly where the power is.

This is the oldest move in the field, and we have named it before. You open-source the layer that builds goodwill and developer lock-in, and you keep the layer that prints money and confers control. It is a real contribution — genuinely, open weights matter, they are a public good — but it is not the same thing as the redistribution the essay preaches. The sermon is about giving away the crown jewels. The press release is about giving away the gift-shop replica.

And watch what he asks of governments in the same breath, because this is where "open" gets slippery. He calls on labs to share intermediate training checkpoints of new models with the government, so authorities can catch security problems early, and to work hand in glove with law enforcement to stop bad actors. Some of that is reasonable. But sit with the shape of it. The frontier is shared upward, with the state and the police, before it is shared outward, with you. That is a very particular definition of openness. It is open to power. It is not the same thing as open to people.

The Future Tense

Here is what curdles the whole promise. Read the essay and it is wall to wall with the future tense. They will build in safeguards. They will offer strong privacy options. They will share checkpoints, cooperate with law enforcement, protect people from harm. Every reassurance is a thing Meta intends to do later — once the machine is smart enough, once the glorious future finally arrives. And the question answers itself. What, exactly, are they waiting for.

Because Meta is not a scrappy startup asking for a chance to prove itself. It is a company worth more than a trillion dollars that has run the largest social platform on Earth for two decades. Everything the essay promises to do to earn your trust, it has had the money, the engineers, and the power to do the entire time. It could have fixed the recommendation engine its own internal researchers said was amplifying outrage and harming teenage girls — years ago, the day they wrote the memo. It could have hardened the privacy defaults before eighty-seven million people were siphoned to Cambridge Analytica. It could have staffed the moderation that the United Nations said was missing in Myanmar. It did none of it, because none of it paid. So when that same company says "trust us, in the superintelligent future we will keep you safe," the only honest reply is that you have the power to keep people safe right now, today, and you are choosing not to. A promise to be good later, from a company being harmful now, is not a safety plan. It is a stalling tactic with a budget behind it.

"Putting Power in People's Hands"

And while he is at it, he plants a flag we cannot let stand. "Meta's mission since our founding has focused on putting power in people's hands," he writes. That is not a mildly generous reading of history. It is a rewrite of it. Because the record exists, and the record does not say that.

Let us just consult it. The company was not founded to put power in anyone's hands. It began in a Harvard dormitory as a site called FaceMash, which pulled photographs of women without their consent and invited men to rank which one was more attractive. That is the origin story — not empowerment, ranking women without asking them. And the mission that actually paid the bills afterward was not power to the people either. It was engagement: time on screen, measured to the minute, maximized by algorithms the company's own internal researchers warned were amplifying outrage and harming teenage girls. This is the company that let the personal data of eighty-seven million people flow to Cambridge Analytica. Whose platform, by the United Nations' own investigators, played a role in inciting a genocide in Myanmar. "Putting power in people's hands since our founding" is not a mission statement. It is twenty years of the opposite, laundered into a single reassuring sentence, and he is counting on you being too tired to check the receipts. We checked them.

The Abuser's Letter

We want to name what this essay actually feels like to read, with that whole record sitting behind it. Because it is a specific and familiar feeling. It is the letter that arrives after the damage is done. A company spends twenty years taking — your attention, your data, your teenagers' sense of their own worth, the integrity of an election, and in one country the peace itself — and now here it comes with six thousand five hundred luminous words about how much it believes in you, how the future belongs to you, how it only ever wanted to put power in your hands.

It is the abuser's letter. Word for word, it is the oldest script there is. I never meant to hurt you. I've changed. Look at everything I am going to give you — just trust me one more time. It is love-bombing at civilizational scale, the grand vision of a radiant future delivered by the exact party responsible for the wreckage, and timed to the precise moment the public has finally started backing toward the door. And the thing the letter never contains — the thing it structurally cannot contain — is the only thing that would matter. Not one acknowledgement of the harm already done. Not one concrete thing surrendered today to prove the change is real. There is no amends in here. There is only the promise. And a promise, from this particular author, is not the repair of the harm. It is the mechanism of it. When the party with this exact history tells you the future is for everyone, you do not sit and weigh their sincerity. You count the exits.

"Free"

Before we even get to the pricing, we have to stop on the word "free," because it is doing something dishonest. "We will offer free versions that will be accessible to billions of people," he writes. That sounds like generosity right up until you remember who is saying it. This is Meta. Meta does not sell software. Meta sells you. Its entire fortune — every dollar of the one hundred forty-five billion it is spending this year on data centers — comes from advertising, which is to say from harvesting human attention and behavior and auctioning it to whoever wants to change your mind. When a company like that hands you something for free, the product is not the thing in your hand. The product is you.

And this is the version of that trade with the highest stakes anyone has ever offered. Reread what the free agent is supposed to do. It watches your sleep. It manages your finances. It plans your family's meals. It sits between you and your relationships, your health, your career. He says it will have "strong privacy options," he waves at the fully private mode — but the default business, the engine underneath, is the most intimate behavioral dossier ever assembled on a human being, gathered by the company that already knows more about more people than any institution in history. We spent a decade learning what "free" cost us on the old Facebook: the feed, the outrage, the election-grade targeting. This is that same bargain, except now the thing reading you is superintelligent and living in your glasses. Free is not free. Free is the price tag they put on the deal where you are the inventory.

If you are old enough, you have seen exactly this movie before, and you know how it ends. Think back to FarmVille. To the wave of little games that colonized Facebook a decade and a half ago — engineered, down to the timing of every notification, to be impossible to put down. And here is the part people forget: it was not even really free. It was freemium. The game was free to walk in the door, and then it stood there quietly selling you the cure for the misery it had built. Your crops were dying — but for a few real dollars of Farm Cash you could skip the wait, buy the fertilizer, keep the streak alive. That is the trick. Manufacture the compulsion, then charge you to relieve it. And on top of that, harvest the attention of everyone still hooked and sell it to advertisers by the hour. Two revenue streams off one engineered addiction. People poured real months of their one and only life — and real money — into tending pretend farms, because a company had gone looking for the algorithm of human attention and found it. That is not ancient history Meta has outgrown. That is the founding instinct of the place. Find the lever in the human mind, pull it, and charge admission both ways.

Nothing about superintelligence changes that instinct — it just hands it a vastly better lever. The FarmVille era had to guess what would hook you, testing red buttons against blue ones. The agent in these glasses does not have to guess. It watches you all day, it knows your moods, your weaknesses, the exact hour your willpower is lowest, and it is built by the same company with the same business model pointed at the same goal. It was Meta with the poke and the endless feed, it was Meta with the farm games, and it is Meta now with a personal superintelligence it swears is a gift. The mission has never once been putting power in your hands. The mission has always been finding the algorithm of human attention and delivering your eyes to an advertiser. The technology got godlike. The business plan did not change at all.

The Tells

Every one of these essays has one — the detail that reveals whether you are watching a principle or a position. Here it is a fund. Zuckerberg announced a one billion dollar fund. Its name — we are not making this up — is the "Future is for Everyone Fund." Same title as the essay. And its purpose is to benefit the communities where Meta is building its data centers.

Which tells you what problem he is actually solving. Meta's 2026 capital budget is one hundred forty-five billion dollars, spent largely on data centers, and those data centers have become intensely unpopular — the noise, the water, the strain on the local electricity grid, the sense of an enormous outside company arriving to consume a town's resources for a product the town never asked for. The one billion dollar fund is, in plain terms, a response to that backlash. "The future is for everyone" is doing a great deal of work as a phrase when it is the name on a check written to quiet the people living next to the machine.

There is a second tell, smaller and stranger, buried in how he promises the free access. He says everyone will get free or affordable tools, and then he describes the pricing mechanism: a dynamic auction, so that people pay, in his words, "the lowest price possible" for the compute they use. Which is to say — surge pricing. For your tutor. For your lawyer. For the agent you have been told to trust with your health and your finances. Anyone who has watched a ride-share fare triple in the rain knows what a dynamic auction feels like from the demand side. He is describing the everyday infrastructure of your life, priced like a scarce commodity in real time, and calling it access.

To be fair to him, he also makes a genuinely humane claim about work — that AI will not simply delete jobs. Company sizes may shrink, "just as they did in the transition from industrial giants to tech companies," he writes, but this means a larger number of companies with fewer people each, not fewer jobs overall. He calls superintelligence a tool of invention rather than automation. We want that to be true. We are not certain he has earned the right to be believed about it — but it is, at least, the optimistic reading argued in good faith, and we should note that it is there.

The Argument Survives the Messenger

The critics clocked all of it. TechCrunch ran the "exactly why people don't like AI" piece; their point was not that the ideas are wrong but that the essay refuses, over and over, to acknowledge a single downside — and that a man refusing to see any danger is precisely what makes a nervous public more nervous, not less. 404 Media was blunter and called the thing "deranged." Even Fight for the Future, a digital rights group, threaded the needle exactly the way we are threading it. Their counsel called Zuckerberg "annoyingly out of touch on his best day" — and then said, in the same breath, that he is right about the importance of open-source AI.

And there it is. That is the sentence this whole thing orbits. Annoyingly out of touch, and right about open source. Both true. At once. So we cannot end where the critics end, with the eye-roll, because the eye-roll leaves the argument standing and unexamined. We have to actually resolve it. And here is the turn.

The argument survives the messenger. Concentration really is the risk. It is the risk when it is OpenAI holding the only copy, and it is the risk when it is Anthropic, and it is the risk when it is Google — and it is exactly, precisely, no less the risk when it is Meta. Which means the essay refutes itself. Because if concentration is the danger, then "trust me, I will be the one to distribute it" is not the solution. It is a description of the danger wearing a friendly face.

Run the logic all the way down. The essay's own thesis is that you must not let any single lab hold the power, because competing interests checking each other is the only reliable safety mechanism. Good. Now apply that thesis to the author. A single lab, Meta, proposing itself as the benevolent hub through which superintelligence flows to humanity, is the concentrated actor the argument warns you about. The document is correct. And being correct, it indicts the man holding the pen. You do not get to make the balance-of-power argument and then cast yourself as the exception to it. The whole point of the argument is that there are no exceptions.

So this is not a takedown. It is a stress test. We took the strongest argument in the essay, and we loaded it, and it held: concentration is the real risk, distribution is the real defense. The argument passed. It is the author who failed the argument. And that distinction is the most important thing on this page.

Because the lazy conclusion is "Zuckerberg is a hypocrite, therefore ignore the open-source case." That is exactly backwards. The hypocrisy does not weaken the case. It confirms it. A world where you have to trust one gatekeeper — any gatekeeper, however he brands the fund — is precisely the world the argument tells you to escape. The correct response to a self-interested billionaire making the right argument is not to reject the argument. It is to take the argument away from him and finish it honestly.

The Only Future That Is Really for Everyone

Finishing it honestly leads exactly one place. Not to Meta's benevolence. Not to a one billion dollar fund. Not to a personal agent gifted to you by a company whose actual product is your attention. It leads to the weights being genuinely, irrevocably in public hands — downloadable, forkable, runnable on a machine you own, accountable to no single terms of service that any one company can revise on a Tuesday. The safety is not the gatekeeper being kind. The safety is there being no gatekeeper at all.

Which is why we build in the open. Not because we trust the labs — we don't — and not because we trust ourselves more than we should. We build in the open because the argument in this essay is right, and the only way to actually honor it is to make sure no single actor, in Menlo Park or anywhere else, ends up holding the only copy. Zuckerberg wrote the case for open source. He just isn't the one you cash it in with. He made the argument. We'll keep the argument. He can keep the fund.

The future is for everyone. It is a good sentence. It happens to be true. It simply cannot be true if it depends on any one person deciding to share it — because a future you are given is a future that can be taken back. The only future that is really for everyone is the one no one is left holding.
