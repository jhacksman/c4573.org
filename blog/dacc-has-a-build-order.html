d/acc Has a Build Order

September 18, 2026 · c4573.org

🔊 Listen to this post
Speed:

Four camps are arguing about the future of technology and all four are answering the wrong question.

The effective accelerationists say build everything, as fast as possible, because the universe is on our side. The AI-safety camp says slow down, because the machine may kill everyone. Effective altruism says follow the expected value, which lately means treating frontier AI as the dominant risk to the species. The degrowth left says the project itself is the problem.

Every one of those answers "how fast." Only one framework here answers the question that actually determines outcomes: what do we build first, and why that.

That framework is d/acc. It is not quite three years old, it came out of a blog post rather than an institute, and it is the only position here that hands a working engineer a ranked list. We are going to do this with citations, because some of what gets attached to d/acc does not survive checking, because one of the pieces that does survive was posted two days ago and nobody has read it properly yet, and because we think its author is wrong about two things.

Where the Term Came From

On November 27, 2023, Vitalik Buterin published an essay called "My techno-optimism." It landed a month after Andreessen's techno-optimist manifesto and days after the OpenAI board tried and failed to fire Sam Altman — one side arguing that markets plus technology equals utopia, the other that five people on a nonprofit board should hold the emergency brake for the species. Neither was discussing what to build.

Buterin rejected the axis itself. The anti-technology view, in his summary, is "safety behind, dystopia ahead"; the accelerationist view is "dangers behind, utopia ahead"; his own is "dangers behind, but multiple paths forward ahead: some good, some bad." And the sentence that does the work: "I think that not just magnitude but also direction matters."

Then he named the direction. "The 'd' here can stand for many things; particularly, defense, decentralization, democracy and differential. First, think of it about defense, and then we can see how this ties into the other interpretations."

Thirteen months later, on January 5, 2025, he published "d/acc: one year later" and compressed it to one line: "decentralized and democratic differential defensive acceleration. Build technologies that shift the offense/defense balance toward defense, and do so in a way that does not rely on handing over more power to centralized authorities."

Both halves are load-bearing, and he says why: "any kind of decentralized, democratic or liberal political structure thrives best when defense is easy, and suffers the most challenge when defense is hard." When defending yourself is cheap, you get to be free. When it is expensive, you rent protection from whoever is strongest, and that arrangement has a name and a thousand years of history. His gloss: defense "like in democratic Switzerland and historically quasi-anarchist Zomia, not like the lords and castles of medieval feudalism." Note which of the four d's he leads with and which one does the labor. Defense is legible, so it goes up front. The machinery underneath is differential.

Decentralize, Encrypt, Accelerate

There is a second formulation in circulation, usually recited as three verbs: decentralize, encrypt, accelerate. If you met these ideas through crypto rather than through Buterin's essays, that is probably the version you carry.

It is not the same thing. It is not Buterin's four d's, and we went looking for a named origin and did not find one — no manifesto, no canonical post, no author to credit. So we will describe it accurately: it is the cypherpunk distillation, and it predates the d/acc label by decades. Eric Hughes published "A Cypherpunk's Manifesto" on March 9, 1993: "We must defend our own privacy if we expect to have any." "Cypherpunks write code." And the line that is pure offense-defense reasoning thirty years early: "We know that software can't be destroyed and that a widely dispersed system can't be shut down."

Read that way it is compatible with d/acc rather than in tension with it. Encryption is the cleanest defense-favoring technology anyone has ever shipped — milliseconds to encrypt, geological time to break. The difference is scope: the cypherpunk version already knows what it is building, while d/acc generalizes the criterion to domains that have nothing to do with keys — indoor air, vaccine platforms, resilient supply chains, formal verification, the epistemics of a shared public square. Decentralize, encrypt, accelerate is the opening move, not the whole theory.

"Math-Backed" Is the Wrong Compliment

People who like d/acc sometimes call it math-backed. Be precise about what the structure actually is, because this audience checks. The first pillar is differential technology development, and it is not Buterin's idea. It is Nick Bostrom's, from his 2002 paper on existential risks: "Our focus should be on what I want to call differential technological development: trying to retard the implementation of dangerous technologies and accelerate implementation of beneficial technologies, especially those that ameliorate the hazards posed by other technologies." His worked example was nanotech, and it is the thesis in miniature: "the desirable sequence would be that defense systems are deployed before offensive capabilities become available to many independent powers."

The second pillar is from international relations: Robert Jervis, "Cooperation Under the Security Dilemma," World Politics, volume 30, number 2, January 1978. Two variables decide whether rivals can cooperate without a hegemon — whether offense or defense holds the advantage, and whether an offensive posture can be told apart from a defensive one. The habitable world is the one where defense is cheap and defensive preparations are visibly not threats.

That is a real intellectual spine, and it is not a theorem. There is no proof, and the offense-defense balance is contested inside the field that invented it. Buterin concedes the classification problem himself: nuclear weapons are offense-favoring, nuclear power is neutral, and some technologies sit on both sides depending on the horizon. Ambiguity at the edges, he argues, is an invitation to sharpen the principle rather than an argument against it. Correct, and still not math.

So here is the stronger version of the compliment, which happens to be entirely true. d/acc is the only framework in this argument that specifies what to build first and why. Everything else supplies a throttle setting. This supplies a sequence — and a sequence can be argued with, tested against outcomes, and handed to somebody with a soldering iron.

Mozi Belongs to d/acc, Not to EA

The Warring States period in China was what the name suggests: a few centuries of states trying to eat each other. Into that, in the fifth century BCE, walks Mozi — Mo Di, Master Mo — a figure about whom, by the honest scholarly summary, nothing is reliably known. What the texts say is that he came from a humble family and worked with his hands: the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy notes that early sources depict him as a master craftsman and military engineer, probably an artisan and very likely a carpenter. A tradesman with a doctrine.

Two of that doctrine's ten planks matter here. The first is jian ai, chapters fourteen through sixteen, which the encyclopedia renders as "inclusive care" rather than the older "universal love": concern for everyone, weighted no more heavily for your own family or your own state. The second is fei gong, chapters seventeen through nineteen, "Condemning Offensive Warfare." Aggression is wrong for precisely the reasons theft and murder are wrong — a small gain to the perpetrator paid for by a large loss to the victim.

What the school did with that pair should stop you. They were not pacifists. "Far from being pacifists," the encyclopedia says, "they saw a strong defense force as crucial to state survival," and "some Mohists became specialists in defensive warfare, rushing to the aid of cities and states under threat of attack." The last block of the Mozi is not philosophy at all. Chapters fifty-two to seventy-one are military engineering: fortification specifications, counters to scaling ladders and tunneling and battering rams, addressed as answers to his chief disciple Qin Guli. Engineers with an ethic, who would help you hold a wall and would not help you breach one.

Before we lean on that, the caveats, because this material is thinner than a confident paragraph makes it sound. Of those twenty-odd military chapters only eleven survive, badly corrupted. No part of the Mozi claims to be from Mo Di's own hand, and the defense chapters were most likely written down by Qin Guli's students. The famous anecdotes — Mozi talking the ruler of Chu out of attacking Song — sit in a layer scholars date later and call probably partly fictional. What is attested is a school and a technical literature, not a hero's biography. That is enough for the argument, and we are not going to inflate it into a legend.

Now the part we expected to have to argue for, and do not. Neither d/acc essay mentions Mozi, the Mohists, or any Chinese philosopher — we checked both, and the count is zero. But on September 17, two days before this post went up, Buterin made the connection himself, publicly, on Farcaster. He made it twice in twenty-six seconds, and aimed it differently each time.

The first post aimed it at effective altruism: Mozi was "surprisingly close to being a proto-effective-altruist," on the strength of "universal love" plus "being efficient about how you use your limited resources to most effectively help people." That is the cast the crypto press picked up and reprinted. Then, two replies down his own thread, he corrected the aim: "Mozi is also surprisingly proto d/acc (!!)" — because the defense chapters "are basically open-source city defense manuals," and because "the explicit goal was to upgrade people's knowledge of defensive arts, and thus shift the offense/defense balance further toward defense." The aggregators did not reprint that one.

The second aim is the right one, and it is not close. The resemblance to effective altruism stops at the standard: impartial benefit, everyone counted once. It does not extend to the method. Mohist practice was not expected-value estimation across cause areas; it was fortification engineering, written down and handed out. The doctrine says aggression is theft, and the corpus makes theft unprofitable. Publishing the specifications so any city could hold its wall is open-source defensive infrastructure, and it works for the reason encryption works: it raises the attacker's cost far more than the defender's.

Which sharpens the point about the movement claiming him. Effective altruism takes the ethics and arrives at compute governance, licensing thresholds and a short list of trusted labs. The Mohists took the same premise and arrived at giving away the plans for the wall. Same standard, opposite engineering conclusion — and only one of the two has ever actually moved an offense-defense balance.

The parallel still has a hard limit we are not going to hide. Mohist political doctrine was centralized: "Identifying Upward," a bureaucratic state under a virtuous monarch, everyone aligning their judgments to their superiors. Mozi is an ancestor of the defensive engineering and of the impartial standard. He is not an ancestor of decentralization, and he would probably have found that part irresponsible.

The Case for e/acc, Made Properly

Effective accelerationism was formalized in mid-2022: the original post came from the account known as Swarthy, and the notes that gave the movement its physics came from Beff Jezos, with bayeslord and creatine_cycle as coauthors. In December 2023, Forbes identified the Jezos persona as Guillaume Verdon, a former Google quantum computing researcher who founded Extropic; he has since appeared publicly under both names, so that much is settled.

The argument deserves better than the caricature, because it is a physics argument. Life, Jezos writes, emerged from dissipative adaptation — matter reconfiguring itself to extract free energy, which he traces to the Jarzynski-Crooks fluctuation theorem. The universe therefore "exponentially favors" futures where matter captures more free energy and produces more entropy. Evolution is a special case, intelligence is a faster-timescale specialization, and "capitalism is hence a form of intelligence." Fisher's fundamental theorem then carries the politics: fitness improves in proportion to variance, so regulation that suppresses variance makes civilization less able to respond to anything. And then the line we agree with: "top-down control is not fault-tolerant in its operation to corruption of root nodes in the hierarchy." Central planning is not primarily a moral failure. It is an information failure.

Here is where it collapses. Thermodynamics does not rank. A vaccine platform and an engineered pathogen both dissipate free energy; both are configurations of matter doing what the second law likes. The will of the universe has no opinion about which one you build on Tuesday morning. "Stop fighting the thermodynamic will of the universe. You cannot stop the acceleration. You might as well embrace it. Accelerate." That is the document's conclusion, and as a build order it is empty. It ranks nothing and forbids nothing.

The second problem is a stated commitment rather than an oversight: "e/acc has no particular allegiance to the biological substrate for intelligence and life." As metaphysics that is a position a person can hold; as an engineering priority it is disqualifying, because a framework indifferent to whether humans are still here cannot tell you which failure modes to prevent first. And the third is Buterin's cleanest hit: treating frontier weapons development as a force for good "seems to require believing that the dominant technological power will reliably be one of the good guys in most conflicts, now and in the future." That is a bet on one government's permanent virtue, not a strategy.

The Case for the Safety Camp, Made Properly

Effective altruism and the AI-safety movement have a stronger claim here than their critics admit, starting with the fact that they supplied the spine. Differential technology development is their concept, and Buterin says so himself.

They also produced the best objection to d/acc, which Buterin published against himself: "it's looking likely we have three year timelines until AGI, and another three years until superintelligence," so "we can't just accelerate the good, we also have to slow down the bad, and this means passing powerful regulations that may make powerful people upset." He concedes his first essay had no answer, then offers two: liability placed as close to end use as possible, and a soft-pause capability able to cut worldwide compute by ninety to ninety-nine percent for a year or two, via trusted hardware that stops running unless it collects three signatures a week from international bodies.

The failure is structural, and it appears every time this camp converts a concern into policy: the answer collapses toward centralized control. Sam Altman, before the Senate on May 16, 2023, proposed "a combination of licensing and testing requirements for development and release of AI models above a threshold of capabilities." Export controls carry a we-trust-our-side-not-yours logic by construction. Aschenbrenner proposes the United States race to a decisive lead and then dictate terms to China. And the floor of the genre sits in the literature Buterin quotes: Bostrom's scenario in which everyone wears a "freedom tag" that continuously uploads encrypted video and audio for machine interpretation in real time.

What the center never prices is the risk that the center is the problem. Buterin lists the receipts: gain-of-function research funded by major world governments as a plausible origin of the pandemic, and the World Health Organization declining for years to acknowledge that Covid was airborne. Centralized epistemology failed in the exact scenario it was built for.

None of this makes them villains. It makes them predictable. Give this camp everything it asks for and the artifact it produces is a chokepoint — offense-favoring by construction, because whoever captures it can aim it.

What a Build Order Looks Like on a Workbench

Now the operational part, which is the reason any of this matters. Buterin's triage for AI risk is three lines long and it is a ranked list: "It hacks our computers, cyber-defense. It creates a super-plague, bio-defense. It convinces us, either to trust it, or to distrust each other, info-defense."

Read that as a work queue and it tells you things. A formal verification toolchain outranks a bigger model. Sandboxing and egress monitoring outrank a policy document about responsible scaling. Clean indoor air outranks another benchmark. Tooling that lets a crowd annotate a claim in public outranks another centralized moderation regime. The 2025 follow-up lists what shipped in a year under that banner: verifiable open-source vaccines, healthy indoor air, Community Notes, prediction markets maturing as an information tool, zero-knowledge proofs in government ID.

The design principle underneath is the best sentence in either essay: focus on "building mecha suits for the human mind, not on creating new forms of self-sustaining intelligent life."

Here is why this framework survives contact with people who ship, and it is not a philosophical reason. Nearly every item on that list can be built by someone who is not a frontier lab. Air filtration is a fan, a filter and arithmetic. Open vaccine protocols are a publication. Sandboxing and audit logs are a weekend and some discipline. You cannot build a licensing regime in a hackerspace. You can build almost every defensive technology in one, publish the plans, and have ten thousand people holding it by Friday. Which is, to the letter, what the Mohists did with the fortification manuals.

Where We Part Company

This is not a fan letter, so: two disagreements. The soft pause fails its own test — industrial hardware that will not run without three signatures a week from international bodies is a global chokepoint with better ergonomics, and by the framework's own reasoning it turns offense-favoring the moment anyone captures it. The artifact is the problem, not its governance.

The second is the hedge on open weights. He writes that there is "a high chance that the most powerful models will be closed source anyway." As a prediction it may be right; as a plan it is unacceptable, because every defense built downstream of a closed frontier is built on a landlord's terms. If the offense-defense balance decides whether people stay free, the weights are defensive infrastructure and they belong in public — published, like the fortification specs. Not communism, not socialism: bootstrapism. The people who tell you to pull yourself up by your bootstraps are standing on the bootstraps.

Pick the Direction

The goal has not changed since the first post on this site: post-scarcity, and the end of the enormous share of human suffering that exists only because the things people need are still expensive. That requires speed, and every year of delay has a body count the people asking for delay never put on their own ledger.

But speed alone does not pick a destination. Speed in an offense-favoring world produces a warlord, because there the cheapest way to be safe is to be the biggest threat. Speed in a defense-favoring world produces something closer to Switzerland with better tools. Same throttle, different sequence, completely different century. That is why d/acc beats the alternatives without needing to be a theorem: e/acc says floor it and offers no map, the safety camp hands you a map whose only road ends at a checkpoint, and d/acc says floor it in a direction — build the shield before the sword, publish the plans, refuse to create the chokepoints.

Mozi had the doctrine right twenty-four centuries ago and lost anyway. His school could rush engineers to one besieged city at a time, carrying fortification plans by cart, and when the Mohists faded the manuals sat unread for two thousand years. We can copy a defensive design to every city on Earth before dinner. That is the genuinely new variable, and it is why the build order can win this time.

Accelerate. Pick the direction. The only way out is through.
