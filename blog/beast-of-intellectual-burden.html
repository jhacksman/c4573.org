The Beast of Intellectual Burden

October 8, 2026 · c4573.org

🔊 Listen to this post
Speed:

On October 5, Benjamin Riley published an essay in The Verge arguing that AI is a cognitive hot dog. The analogy is good, and it is worth stating properly before anyone argues with it. Fatty food was scarce and precious for most of human history, so we evolved to crave it and store it. Then agriculture made it plentiful. The craving outlived its usefulness and started killing us. Riley's claim is that effortful thinking follows the same arc: hard, necessary, and now trivially avoidable. Put a chatbot in front of a child and the child takes the off-ramp. The arteries in question are cognitive.

He is half right. The half he gets right is the half that matters least for policy. A child who delegates every difficult step learns less, and that is not a hypothesis anymore. A model that invents a citation for a student who has no way to check citations has served something worse than empty calories. Both of those are real. They are also externalities of a particular product, sold a particular way, pointed at a child with nobody watching. Riley's prescription treats them as properties of the capability itself, and that is a different claim that does not follow from his evidence.

So here is an older question. Roughly six thousand years ago, somebody yoked cattle to a plow and stopped doing the pulling. Was the beast of burden bad for civilization? And if it was not, is a beast of intellectual burden bad today?

The Diagnosis Is Half Right

Start by giving the concern everything it is owed, because the research Riley cites is not decoration. He builds on Paul Cisek's work at the University of Montreal, which treats brains as feedback-control systems rather than input-output machines: organisms act on the world to change what they perceive, and the nervous system is a record of control extending further outward over evolutionary time. If that is closer to right than the computational model, then a tutor that supplies finished answers is not feeding a processor. It is removing the loop the child was supposed to close.

The empirical side is worse than the theory suggests. A study out of China found thousands of students essentially stopped doing homework once they had access. Other work indicates that students who lean on these systems become habituated to relaxing their judgment in unrelated contexts. Researchers have a name for it now, cognitive delegation, and their warning has a nasty shape: as delegation spreads, the social environment that supports unaided reasoning weakens, which makes further delegation easier and more attractive. A ratchet, not a slope.

The sharpest version of the worry is not about gaps in knowledge. It is about calibration. A student who has never personally caught the machine lying has no internal model of when to doubt it. Confident error is a tolerable property of a tool used by someone with the judgment to catch it, and a corrosive property of the same tool handed to someone building that judgment for the first time. We are not going to pretend otherwise. Anyone who has watched a smart adult accept a fabricated reference should be able to picture a twelve year old doing it.

The Ox Was Also a Shortcut

Now the other side of the ledger. Somewhere in the fourth millennium before the common era, farmers in Mesopotamia harnessed cattle to a scratch plow. The gain was not marginal. A person with a hoe feeds a household. A person behind an ox team feeds a village, and the difference between those two numbers is the grain surplus that paid for cities, for cuneiform, for scribes, and eventually for the institutions Riley wants to defend. Formal education is downstream of the plow. Schools exist because an animal took over work a human used to do.

Something was lost. Humans did get weaker at pulling. Whatever intimacy with soil and body a farmer had before the yoke, some of it went away and did not come back. That objection was not wrong. It was simply answered at a different level than it was raised, because we stopped measuring a civilization by how much weight a man could drag and started measuring it by what the village could build with the time.

Riley preempts the obvious rebuttal and he deserves credit for it. He is tired of people throwing Plato's complaint about writing at him, and he is right that the complaint is overused and right that writing is not a chatbot. So we will not argue from Plato. The ox is a different argument. It is not the claim that people worried before and were wrong. It is the claim that offloading a faculty has repeatedly been the mechanism by which the faculty's output got amplified, and that the amplification shows up one level above the thing that was lost.

The calculator is the recent case and it does not favor either side cleanly. Mental arithmetic fluency did decline. Mathematics education did move toward modeling and interpretation, which is more than a consolation prize. Nobody got there by banning the device. They got there by deciding which steps a student still performs unaided and which ones the machine is allowed to carry, and then teaching to that line. That decision is pedagogy. It requires a line, not a prohibition.

Where the Plow Analogy Breaks

We should say plainly where our own frame fails, because it fails in at least four places and a reader who spots them before we do is entitled to discount everything after.

An ox replaces muscle, and thinking is not downstream of muscle. It may well be downstream of struggle, which is exactly the faculty at issue. The plow also has no opinion. It cannot hand you a confident falsehood, and no ox ever hallucinated a furrow. Those two disanalogies are not cosmetic. They are the reason a tool that substitutes for cognition is a genuinely new category rather than another entry in a long list of labor-saving devices.

Third, a child is not a farmer. The farmer already knew what a field was for, what a bad harvest looked like, and when the animal was going the wrong way. A seven year old producing a paragraph about photosynthesis is not delegating a solved task. They are skipping the construction of the apparatus that would let them notice the paragraph is wrong.

Fourth, and this one cuts closest: the time-saved argument is an adult's argument. We find it persuasive for ourselves because we already possess the judgment the tool is standing in for, so the hours we recover are real hours. A student has no such reserve to draw against. Time saved on the one thing you are supposed to be building is not savings. It is skipped construction, and the bill arrives later with interest.

So Riley's floor is correct. There is an age, and a set of uses, below which the hot dog framing simply holds. We do not know precisely where that line sits, and neither does anyone else writing about this. Norway put it at thirteen. New York City put it at the end of eighth grade. We have no better evidence than they do and we are not going to pretend to. What we dispute is not the floor. It is who gets to hold the switch.

Name the Lords

In a July 2025 podcast, OpenAI's vice president of education, Leah Belsky, said that more than half of the roughly 900 million average monthly users of ChatGPT were learners, and called the service the world's largest learning platform. Read that as a market disclosure rather than a boast. A company counted children's homework, found it was the majority of its traffic, and said so on a podcast.

On July 14, 2026, Anthropic launched Claude for Teachers. Verified teachers in the United States get free premium access, a library of teaching skills, and lesson materials aligned to academic standards in all fifty states. Model training is off by default on educator accounts. Sign up by June 30, 2027 and the free year is yours. The list price is twenty dollars a month. A separate product for schools and districts is, in the company's own words, under development.

We take no position on whether anyone involved is sincere. We observe the structure. The free tier runs a year. The standards alignment work covers fifty states. The district product is where the revenue is. By the time a teacher's trial lapses, their lesson plans, their accumulated student context, and their working habits live inside one vendor's projects, and the district that wants to keep any of it will be negotiating from inside that dependency rather than outside it. This is the tenancy pattern we described in Three Companies Control Commoditized Intelligence. The classroom version is worse in one specific respect. The tenant is a public institution spending public money, and the thing on the meter is a child's instruction.

If you want to see how little leverage the tenant has, look at how the bans were implemented. Los Angeles Unified restricted student access for the current school year by having Gemini, AI Mode in Search, and the assistants inside Google Classroom, Docs, Sheets and Slides disabled on school accounts. The second largest district in the country exercised its sovereignty over classroom technology by filing a configuration request with a vendor. That is the whole of the instrument available to it.

A Ban Is a Switch Someone Else Owns

The prohibitions are real and they are spreading. On June 19, 2026, Norway's prime minister announced a near total restriction for grades one through seven, ages six to thirteen, effective at the start of the school year in late August. Students aged fourteen to sixteen may use these tools cautiously under a teacher's supervision. Students aged seventeen to nineteen are to be taught how to use them properly. Of the three policies we are about to list, Norway's is the best shaped, because it is graduated and it ends in instruction rather than silence.

On September 2, 2026, New York City announced a one year moratorium on student-facing generative AI through eighth grade, including companion chatbots, with twice yearly literacy classes for high school students. Los Angeles Unified went further in scope, covering all grades, with an ad hoc committee due to report policy recommendations to the school board by the end of the school year.

We have argued since You Can't Ban Math that fear leads to bans and bans lead to irrelevance. That holds, with one amendment we owe these districts: a one year moratorium with a committee and a reporting date is not a ban on mathematics. It is an institution buying time to write a policy it does not yet have. That is a legitimate use of a pause and we will say so plainly.

The failure mode is not the pause. It is a pause in which nothing gets built. A moratorium that expires back into the same procurement conversation has not protected anyone's cognition. It has deferred the tenancy by twelve months and handed the vendors a year to prepare a district offering, which they are doing right now, on the record. The intent is sincere. The fable is familiar. The outcome is a purchase order.

State the category error without decoration. The harms Riley documents belong to a rented, engagement-shaped, unauditable chatbot aimed at an unsupervised child. Prohibit that. The capability is a different object: a patient explainer that runs inside the building, returns the same answer twice, keeps no record anywhere else, and cannot be repriced in the middle of a semester. Confusing the two is how a school ends up with neither. Govern the externality, keep the capability, the same argument we made about doom generally.

And nobody is coming. There is no federal standard arriving, no publicly funded vendor-neutral tutor being built for the districts that want one, no rescue. The committee will report. The vendors will have a proposal ready that week.

What a School Could Own Instead

Here is the alternative, concretely, because a prescription that stays abstract is just a mood. Open weights, local inference, one machine the school owns.

A workstation with a single consumer accelerator runs an open-weight model well enough to explain a fraction problem, walk a student through a lab writeup, or translate a reading passage for a family that speaks another language at home. It costs money once. It sits in a closet. It has no outbound connection, which means there is no telemetry to audit because there is no telemetry.

What that buys is not primarily privacy, although it buys that too. The system prompt belongs to the teacher. The logs stay in the building, which means a parent asking what the machine told their child gets an answer from the school rather than a support ticket. It works when the network does not. It cannot be repriced, deprecated, or retuned out from under a lesson plan in the middle of a unit. And it does not optimize for session length, because nobody's revenue depends on the child coming back tomorrow.

The part that matters most is the part no vendor will ever ship. A model you hold can be deliberately crippled for pedagogy. Configure it to answer only with questions. Configure it to refuse to produce finished prose. Require a student to state a claim before it will discuss the topic at all. Cap it at three exchanges per assignment. Those are all one file on a machine you control, and every one of them is a product decision a company optimizing for engagement has no reason to make.

Then there is verification, which is the whole ballgame. The researchers Riley cites recommend preserving unaided problem solving, verification, critical discussion, and deliberate disengagement. Three of those four get easier when the weights are yours, because you can make the model lie on purpose. A teacher who can seed a specific false claim into a local model and hand the class the job of catching it has a calibration lesson no commercial product will ever sell, for the obvious reason. That lesson is the actual answer to the hallucination problem. Not abstinence. Target practice.

This is the build order we laid out in d/acc Has a Build Order, applied to a classroom. Identify the specific harm. Build the defense people can own. Check who holds the result when you are finished.

Hot Dog or Plow

Riley is right that a diet of hot dogs ruins a body, and right that nobody should be feeding them to children at scale because a vendor has a growth target. He is wrong that the remedy is to keep the animal out of the field. Both halves of that are true at once, and the gap between them is where the next decade of school budgets will be spent.

Children are going to grow up with a beast of intellectual burden in the barn. That was decided by the price of inference, not by a school board. The only question still open is whether the school owns the animal or rents it by the token.

Buy the weights. Run them in the building. Teach the kids to catch it lying.
