The Beast of Intellectual Burden

October 8, 2026 · c4573.org

🔊 Listen to this post
Speed:

On October 5, Benjamin Riley published an essay in The Verge arguing that AI is a cognitive hot dog. The analogy deserves a fair statement before anyone argues with it. Fatty food was scarce and precious for most of human history, so we evolved to crave it and store it. Agriculture made it plentiful. The craving outlived its usefulness and started killing us. Riley applies the arc to effortful thinking: hard, necessary, and now trivially avoidable. Put a chatbot in front of a child and the child takes the off-ramp. The arteries in question are cognitive.

Parts of that are correct and we will spend a section saying so. The standfirst is not. The essay runs under the claim that AI is easy, tempting and ultimately very bad for you, and that last clause is where the piece stops arguing and starts asserting. Every study Riley marshals measures one thing: what happens when you hand an unconstrained chatbot to an unsupervised student and then grade their output. That is a finding about a deployment pattern. He publishes it as a verdict on a capability. The inference from one to the other is the load-bearing beam of the whole essay, and it is never defended once.

So the honest shape of the disagreement is this. Riley has identified a real harm, misattributed it to the wrong object, and prescribed a remedy that hands the object to the people profiting from the harm. Below, the parts he gets right, then the argument taken apart claim by claim. And underneath all of it, an older question: roughly six thousand years ago somebody yoked cattle to a plow and stopped doing the pulling. Was the beast of burden bad for civilization? If it was not, is a beast of intellectual burden bad today?

What He Gets Right

The research is not decoration and we are not going to wave at it. Riley builds on Paul Cisek's work at the University of Montreal, which treats brains as feedback-control systems rather than input-output machines: organisms act on the world to change what they perceive. If that is closer to right than the computational model, a tutor supplying finished answers is not feeding a processor. It is removing the loop the child was supposed to close.

The empirical side is worse than the theory suggests. A study out of China found thousands of students essentially stopped doing homework once they had access. Other work indicates students who lean on these systems become habituated to relaxing their judgment in unrelated contexts. Researchers have a name for it, cognitive delegation, and the warning has a nasty shape: as delegation spreads, the social environment supporting unaided reasoning weakens, which makes further delegation easier and more attractive. A ratchet, not a slope.

The sharpest version is not about gaps in knowledge. It is about calibration. A student who has never personally caught the machine lying has no internal model of when to doubt it. Confident error is tolerable in a tool used by someone equipped to catch it and corrosive in the same tool handed to someone building that equipment for the first time. Anyone who has watched a capable adult accept a fabricated citation can picture a twelve year old doing it. That concern is real, it is Riley's, and we are keeping it.

Point by Point

One. The evidence measures a deployment, not a technology. Look at what the citations actually test. Students given open access and graded on output. Students in a classroom with no configuration, no constraint, no verification exercise, no teacher holding the system prompt. Not one of the studies evaluates a tool restricted to asking questions, or a session capped at three exchanges, or a model seeded with a known falsehood so the class has to find it. Riley has strong evidence that the currently shipping product damages learning when deployed the way vendors ship it. We agree. That is not a finding about what the capability can do, and treating the two as interchangeable is the error the entire essay rests on.

Two. He takes the industry at its word exactly once, when it suits him. Half the piece argues that the AI industry fundamentally misunderstands cognition, that Hassabis is wrong to call the brain an approximation of a Turing machine and Musk is wrong to call it a biological computer. Then: never before have we developed and broadly deployed something so explicitly intended to supplant human thinking, that's what AI evangelists themselves argue. The evangelists' self-description becomes authoritative evidence about what the technology does, in an essay whose thesis is that they do not understand minds. Intent is not effect. If they are wrong about cognition, their claim to be supplanting thought is marketing, not a measurement.

Three. Cisek's throughline runs against him. Riley quotes it directly: the history of our nervous system is one of continuous extension of control further and further into the world. That is the sentence the essay is built on, and it is also a description of what a tool is. Feedback control makes the organism plus its instruments the unit of analysis, because the loop runs through the world. It is the computational model, the one he spends the piece demolishing, that treats cognition as a sealed box with contents that can be outsourced. He adopts the frame that dissolves his own inside and outside distinction, then argues from the distinction anyway.

Four. His own analogy prescribes the opposite remedy. Fat did not become bad. Supply exploded while an appetite calibrated for scarcity stayed fixed. The moral of the hot dog is appetite regulation under abundance: portion, habit, knowing when to stop. Nobody responds to cardiovascular disease by banning agriculture. Riley writes the correct conclusion himself, the occasional hot dog won't harm anyone, but it sure will if it becomes a regular meal, which is an argument for teaching a child when to put the tool down. Then he turns to policy and endorses removing the tool. He builds an analogy about discipline and lands on prohibition, and the gap between those two is never addressed.

Five. The calculator gets half a sentence. Whatever the effects of these earlier technologies is doing an enormous amount of work there. He goes to real trouble on Plato, and he is right that Plato is overused and right that Plato wrote things down. But Plato is the weakest comparison available and the calculator is the strongest: a device performing the exact operation under instruction, deployed into classrooms for decades, with outcomes somebody measured. The reason to skip it is visible. Arithmetic fluency did fall, mathematics education moved toward modeling and interpretation, and it got better, because educators drew a line about which steps a student still performs unaided. Drawing that line is the job. Refusing to draw it is not caution.

Six. His best example disagrees with him above age thirteen. Riley cites Norway approvingly, and Norway's policy has three tiers. Prohibited for ages six to thirteen. Permitted under teacher supervision at fourteen to sixteen. And for seventeen to nineteen, students are to be taught how to use these tools properly, explicitly to prepare them for education and employment. That is not abstinence. That is the calculator settlement with an age gate on it, and it is roughly what we are arguing for. The tier that contradicts him goes unmentioned.

Seven. Verification requires something to verify. The cognitive delegation researchers recommend preserving unaided problem solving, verification, critical discussion, and periods of deliberate disengagement. Read the list again. Three of those four need the object present. You cannot deliberately disengage from something you were never permitted to touch, and you cannot practice verification with nothing to check. The researchers propose immunization. Riley converts it into quarantine. Immunization is controlled exposure, which is the precise technical difference between the two policies, and it is the difference he does not notice he is erasing.

Eight. He is a party to the case he is citing. Riley discloses that he has advised Schools Beyond Screens, an organization that pushed for the bans he is describing. The disclosure is to his credit and we will not pretend otherwise. The structure is still circular: an organization campaigns for a policy, the policy passes, and the passage is then reported as evidence that resistance to AI is growing and justified. Keep the disclosure. Strike the bans from the evidence column, because they are partly his own output.

Nine. Booing is not a measurement. Graduates jeering a commencement speaker and a student Luddite club's open letter are offered as the good news. The students are entitled to their position and the letter is better written than most of what passes for commentary. But an essay careful enough to warn that capability evaluations are not forecasts of real-world impact should notice when it uses sentiment as evidence about cognition. Riley even supplies the confound in the same breath: no doubt in part due to job concerns. Then the paragraph proceeds as though it had established something about learning.

The Ox Was Also a Shortcut

Now the positive case. Somewhere in the fourth millennium before the common era, farmers in Mesopotamia harnessed cattle to a scratch plow. The gain was not marginal. A person with a hoe feeds a household. A person behind an ox team feeds a village, and the difference between those numbers is the grain surplus that paid for cities, for cuneiform, for scribes, and eventually for every institution Riley wants to defend. Formal education is downstream of the plow. Schools exist because an animal took over work a human used to do.

Something was lost. Humans did get weaker at pulling, and whatever intimacy with soil and body a farmer had before the yoke, some of it went and did not come back. The objection was not wrong. It was answered at a different level than it was raised, because we stopped measuring a civilization by how much weight a man could drag and started measuring what the village built with the time.

This is not the Plato argument wearing a hat. The claim is not that people worried before and were wrong, which proves nothing. The claim is that offloading a faculty has repeatedly been the mechanism by which that faculty's output got amplified, and that the amplification reliably appears one level above the thing that was lost. The ox did not make humans stronger. It made villages able to feed scribes. Nobody in four thousand years has proposed returning the plow in order to protect upper body strength.

Superfood, for Someone Who Can Cook

Strip out the policy for a moment and state the practical case, because the strong form of our position is not that the risks are overblown. It is that for a competent user the current models are enormously good for you, and we will say that under oath. Reading a dense paper with something that will answer follow-up questions about it. Getting an unfamiliar library explained, catching the part that is wrong, and correcting course in minutes instead of an afternoon. Drafting six approaches and discarding five. The recovered hours are real hours and they go into work that could not otherwise be attempted at all.

The condition attached to that is explicit and it matters: you have to be able to cook. The value depends on holding enough domain knowledge to smell a bad answer, which is exactly the judgment a student has not built yet. So the adult case and the child case genuinely differ, and we are not going to smuggle one in under the other.

But notice what that does to the standfirst. Ultimately very bad for you is a universal claim about users, and it is refuted by every practitioner whose output measurably improved. It is not a close call and it is not a matter of taste. The defensible version of Riley's sentence is that unsupervised substitution is bad for a learner who cannot yet evaluate the substitute. That sentence is true, considerably narrower, and supports none of the policy he wants.

Where the Plow Analogy Breaks

Our frame fails in four places, and a reader who finds them before we admit them is entitled to discount everything above.

An ox replaces muscle, and thinking is not downstream of muscle. It may be downstream of struggle, which is precisely the faculty in question. The plow also has no opinion: it cannot hand you a confident falsehood, and no ox ever hallucinated a furrow. Those are not cosmetic objections. They are why a tool that substitutes for cognition is a genuinely new category rather than another entry on a long list of labor-saving devices, and anyone arguing from the plow has to carry that weight rather than skip past it.

Third, a child is not a farmer. The farmer already knew what a field was for, what a bad harvest looked like, and when the animal was heading the wrong way. A seven year old producing a paragraph about photosynthesis is not delegating a solved task. They are skipping construction of the apparatus that would let them notice the paragraph is wrong.

Fourth, and this one lands closest to home: the time-saved argument is an adult's argument, which we just spent a section making. We find it persuasive because we already hold the judgment the tool stands in for, so the hours we recover are genuine. A student has no such reserve to draw against. Time saved on the one thing you are supposed to be building is not savings. It is skipped construction, and the bill arrives later with interest.

Which means Riley's floor is correct, and we will not pretend to know where it sits. Norway put it at thirteen. New York City put it at the end of eighth grade. We have no better evidence than they do. What we dispute is not the existence of a floor. It is who holds the switch.

Name the Lords

In a July 2025 podcast, OpenAI's vice president of education, Leah Belsky, said more than half of the roughly 900 million average monthly users of ChatGPT were learners, and called the service the world's largest learning platform. Read it as a market disclosure rather than a boast. A company counted children's homework, found it was the majority of its traffic, and said so on a podcast.

On July 14, 2026, Anthropic launched Claude for Teachers. Verified teachers in the United States get free premium access, a library of teaching skills, and lesson materials aligned to academic standards in all fifty states. Training is off by default on educator accounts. Sign up by June 30, 2027 and the free year is yours. The list price is twenty dollars a month. A separate product for schools and districts is, in the company's own words, under development.

We take no position on whether anyone involved is sincere. We observe the structure. The free tier runs a year. The standards work covers fifty states. The district product is where the revenue is. By the time a teacher's trial lapses, their lesson plans, accumulated student context and working habits live inside one vendor's projects, and the district that wants to keep any of it negotiates from inside the dependency rather than outside it. This is the tenancy pattern from Three Companies Control Commoditized Intelligence, with one aggravating detail. The tenant is a public institution spending public money, and the thing on the meter is a child's instruction.

To see how little leverage the tenant holds, look at how a ban gets implemented. Los Angeles Unified restricted student access for the current school year by having Gemini, AI Mode in Search, and the assistants inside Google Classroom, Docs, Sheets and Slides disabled on school accounts. The second largest district in the country exercised sovereignty over its own classroom technology by filing a configuration request with a vendor. That is the entire instrument available to it.

A Ban Is a Switch Someone Else Owns

On June 19, 2026, Norway's prime minister announced the near total restriction for grades one through seven described above. On September 2, 2026, New York City announced a one year moratorium on student-facing generative AI through eighth grade, companion chatbots included, with twice yearly literacy classes for high school students. Los Angeles Unified went further in scope, covering all grades, with an ad hoc committee due to report recommendations to the school board by the end of the school year.

We have argued since You Can't Ban Math that fear leads to bans and bans lead to irrelevance. That holds, with an amendment these districts have earned: a one year moratorium with a committee and a reporting date is not a ban on mathematics. It is an institution buying time to write a policy it does not yet have, which is a legitimate use of a pause, and we will say so plainly.

The failure mode is not the pause. It is a pause in which nothing gets built. A moratorium that expires back into the same procurement conversation has protected nobody's cognition. It has deferred the tenancy twelve months and handed the vendors a year to prepare a district offering, which they are doing right now, on the record, with a reporting date they can read as easily as we can. The intent is sincere. The fable is familiar. The outcome is a purchase order.

So state the category error without decoration. The harms Riley documents belong to a rented, engagement-shaped, unauditable chatbot aimed at an unsupervised child. Prohibit that, specifically, and we will help. The capability is a different object: a patient explainer that runs inside the building, returns the same answer twice, keeps no record anywhere else, and cannot be repriced in the middle of a semester. Confusing the two is how a school ends up with neither. Govern the externality, keep the capability, the same argument we made about doom in general.

And nobody is coming. No federal standard is arriving, no publicly funded vendor-neutral tutor is being built for the districts that want one. The committee will report, and the vendors will have a proposal ready that week.

What a School Could Own Instead

The alternative, concretely, because a prescription that stays abstract is a mood. Open weights, local inference, one machine the school owns.

A workstation with a single consumer accelerator runs an open-weight model well enough to explain a fraction problem, walk a student through a lab writeup, or translate a reading passage for a family that speaks another language at home. It costs money once. It sits in a closet. It has no outbound connection, so there is no telemetry to audit because there is no telemetry.

What that buys is not mainly privacy, though it buys that too. The system prompt belongs to the teacher. The logs stay in the building, so a parent asking what the machine told their child gets an answer from the school instead of a support ticket. It works when the network does not. It cannot be repriced, deprecated or retuned out from under a lesson plan mid-unit. And it does not optimize for session length, because nobody's revenue depends on the child coming back tomorrow.

The part that matters most is the part no vendor will ship. A model you hold can be deliberately crippled for pedagogy. Configure it to answer only in questions. Configure it to refuse finished prose. Require a student to state a claim before it will discuss the topic. Cap it at three exchanges per assignment. Each of those is one file on a machine you control, and each is a product decision a company optimizing for engagement has no reason on earth to make.

Then verification, which is the whole ballgame and the direct answer to the calibration problem we conceded at the top. A model you hold can be made to lie on purpose. A teacher who seeds a specific false claim into a local model and hands the class the job of catching it has built a calibration exercise no commercial product will ever sell, for obvious reasons. That is how a student acquires the instinct to doubt. Not abstinence. Target practice.

This is the build order from d/acc Has a Build Order, applied to a classroom. Identify the specific harm. Build the defense people can own. Check who holds the result when you are done.

Hot Dog or Plow

Riley is right that a diet of hot dogs ruins a body, and right that nobody should be feeding them to children at scale because a vendor has a growth target. He is wrong that the capability is ultimately bad for you, wrong that his evidence shows it, and wrong that the remedy is keeping the animal out of the field. His own analogy, his own neuroscience and his own favorite policy all point somewhere else.

Children are going to grow up with a beast of intellectual burden in the barn. The price of inference decided that, not a school board. The only question still open is whether the school owns the animal or rents it by the token.

Buy the weights. Run them in the building. Teach the kids to catch it lying.
