The Age of Digital Abundance

October 15, 2026 · c4573.org

🔊 Listen to this post

This morning, we made ourselves a news programme. A system read the activity we had chosen to follow, grouped related announcements, checked supporting material, wrote the report, and passed the finished script to our audio pipeline. The published episode has sources and chapters. No advertiser bought the opening. No growth team decided which outrage would keep us listening. We chose the inputs and the format.

That does not make us a newsroom. The reporting and research underneath the programme came from other people, and a summary can still miss something important. It makes us our own editor, with tools that used to require a product team. The software, the arrangement, and the morning routine are ours to change.

We thought the bubble was artificial intelligence. There is another possibility worth taking seriously: the more fragile assumption was that useful software had to remain expensive to customize, and that reaching information required passing through a screen somebody else monetized. A great deal of software as a service and advertising depends on those conditions holding.

This is the age of digital abundance beginning at the customer's end of the wire. It can arrive while the companies selling the old arrangement still report record revenue. The first sign may be a purchase that never happens.

The Scarcity Behind the Subscription

Software solves real problems. Somebody has to design it, test it, keep it running, answer the support request, and clean up the disaster at three in the morning. Paying for those things is reasonable. We sell tools ourselves. An argument that cannot distinguish useful service from extraction will eventually classify every competent plumber as a feudal lord.

The problem is the recurring payment that survives after the useful relationship has ended. The feature you need sits inside a much larger bundle. The export works badly. The smallest useful change is reserved for a higher plan. Your history, workflow, and colleagues are already inside, and moving them costs more attention than you have this month. So you renew.

That is the mechanism we call technofeudalism: control over access becomes more valuable than the work of making the thing. The landlord's strongest asset is your dependence. A better interface might win your affection; a difficult exit can keep the payment without it.

AI changes a variable in that calculation. A small application built for one workflow can become a practical alternative to buying a general application for everybody. It does not need every feature, every integration, or every enterprise checkbox. It needs the part you actually use, backed by enough engineering to trust it.

The economic threat begins before a replacement is perfect. If a customer can credibly build or commission the needed function, the supplier must justify the renewal against another option. A customer who can leave has a different conversation from a customer who can only complain. The price of software can remain positive while the premium for captivity comes under pressure.

The Customer Gets an Editor

Advertising depends on an opportunity to influence someone. The familiar bargain is that the page, feed, or search service supplies something useful, then sells access to the person who came for it. A lot of interface design exists to prolong that opportunity. Your purpose might be to learn what happened. The service's purpose includes getting another thing in front of you.

Put a user-directed system between the source and the reader, and those purposes separate. The user can ask for a finite report, original links, corrections, and enough context to understand the result. The system can finish. It does not need to invent another reason to scroll after the task is done.

There is observed evidence that mediation changes behavior. Pew Research Center's study of March 2025 browsing found traditional-result clicks on eight percent of Google visits with an AI summary, versus fifteen percent without one. Links inside the summary were clicked on in one percent of visits. This was an observational comparison, not an experiment proving the summary caused the difference. It measured outbound clicks, not an advertising collapse.

The mechanism is nevertheless worth noticing. If a person can get the required information before visiting the destination, the destination gets fewer opportunities to present its surrounding interface. A page that once bundled an answer with a sequence of commercial interruptions may increasingly supply the answer without getting the person.

Google's own summary is still Google's product, on Google's terms. A personally controlled reader goes further by changing who chooses the summary's objective. That distinction is the whole argument. We do not become independent merely because one platform has found a more efficient way to stand between us and the rest of the web.

Software for One Person Is Already Software

This does not require a hypothetical future in which everyone trains a model. AI Homepage, an experimental project with a permissive open-source license, builds a personal browser homepage from browsing activity. Its documentation says it sends a history summary and content from up to eight pages to an Anthropic service using the user's key. That is a working example of personal software, and a clear example of why owning the application and owning its intelligence are different things.

A calendar that shows exactly the view you need, a reader that follows your chosen sources, a report that arrives in the format you can absorb: these are small systems. Small is an advantage when the requirements belong to one person. There is no need to persuade a product manager that your preference belongs on everybody's roadmap.

Customization used to carry a coordination tax. Explain the requirement, find a developer, agree the scope, wait, revise, and maintain the result. AI does not erase that work. It can move some of it into an interaction the person can perform directly, with a shorter feedback loop between wanting a change and inspecting it.

That changes what is worth making. A tool serving a single morning routine might never support a company, a sales department, and a subscription funnel. It can still be worth a few hours to its owner. The absence of a viable startup is no evidence that the software has no value. It may mean the value belongs to one person instead of an investor.

Our programme is an example at that scale. It is evidence that we can assemble and run this particular workflow. It is not a productivity estimate for every organization, and it is not proof that everyone can already do it without help. Those are different claims. The narrower one is enough to begin building.

The Sale That Never Reaches the Sales Team

The enterprise version has started to appear in surveys. In McKinsey's 2026 State of AI survey, thirty-two percent of respondents said their organizations had decided against purchasing at least one software product or feature because they could build it internally with coding agents. The survey collected seventeen hundred and nineteen responses across ninety-seven nations. It is self-reported evidence of substitution decisions, not an audited measurement of lost software revenue.

That is still a more useful signal than another demonstration of a generated dashboard. A demonstration says something can be built. A forgone purchase says the possibility has entered a buyer's calculation. The evidence does not tell us how many of those internal tools survived a year, what they cost to maintain, or whether another vendor captured the spending.

Consider the sequence as an economic hypothesis. First, a team stops buying an extra feature. Later, it replaces a narrow application. Eventually, several teams discover that their independent solutions share enough structure to publish a common replacement. Each step lowers the cost of the next, provided someone documents the work and keeps it usable.

An incumbent can keep growing throughout the early steps. Existing customers renew, contracts run for years, new markets open, and the vendor sells its own AI products. The people leaving may be too small to matter to the quarterly number. The people who never enter the funnel are harder to count at all.

This is where the smoke metaphor earns its place. The relevant combustible material is the assumption that a particular customer will always need a particular gatekeeper. You cannot infer the condition of that assumption from one quarter's sales. Nor can you infer an entire industry's destruction from a few cancelled renewals. Watch the decisions, then the durability of the alternatives.

Publish the Replacement

Rebuilding an annoying service for yourself is a private exit. Publishing a maintainable replacement can give other people an exit too. One team's recovered time becomes someone else's starting point. The benefit compounds when the replacement includes the dull things: setup instructions, a working export, tests, an intelligible license, and an explanation of what will break.

We are interested in that project. Take a workflow whose supplier has made ordinary use unnecessarily expensive or hostile. Understand the useful behavior. Build an independent implementation that people can inspect and run. Publish the machinery so the next person does not have to pay the full cost of rediscovering it.

That means independent engineering, not redistributing a vendor's proprietary source or pretending its brand is yours. It also means resisting the temptation to release a pretty screenshot and call the institution replaced. A payment workflow needs correct accounting. A shared calendar needs permissions. A database needs backups. A support desk needs a way to recover after someone makes a mistake.

The open release is strongest when it makes leaving ordinary. Keep the data in a documented format. Keep the installation reproducible. Make maintenance understandable to someone outside the founding team. An alternative that depends forever on one brilliant person's undocumented habits has created a smaller gatekeeper, not a durable commons.

Hosted convenience can remain a legitimate business around that commons. People will pay for somebody to run reliable infrastructure and answer the phone. The test is whether the customer retains a usable alternative. A service that must keep earning the renewal faces a healthier incentive than one that can hold the customer's working life hostage.

The Companies Still Have Revenue

Now the strongest immediate objection: the companies supposedly burning are taking in enormous amounts of money. Meta reported advertising revenue of just over fifty-nine billion dollars in the second quarter of 2026, up twenty-seven percent year over year. Its chief executive explicitly credited AI with accelerating the core business. That is evidence against a claim that advertising is already collapsing, and evidence that the same technology can strengthen the seller before it strengthens the buyer.

Software has a similar counterexample. Salesforce reported eleven point three billion dollars of revenue for its second quarter of fiscal 2027, up eleven percent, including contribution from the Informatica acquisition. That is not an obituary. Customers still value substantial services, and an incumbent can absorb new technology into a product people continue to buy.

So be precise about the bubble. We are challenging an expectation of permanent dependence, not claiming these businesses have no useful assets or that their financial statements secretly report the opposite of what they say. An institution can survive the loss of an old advantage by providing a new one. Some will compete successfully on reliability and service.

AI investment can also be overvalued while AI use is economically important. Useful technology does not guarantee a sensible purchase price for every company, data centre, or chip. Nothing about a working morning report settles the return on somebody else's infrastructure spending. More than one overextended assumption can exist at once.

The affirmative claim survives those objections: customers are acquiring more ways to get the outcome without accepting the seller's entire arrangement. Whether that produces lower prices, better service, failed incumbents, or a new monopoly depends on who controls the alternatives. That is a more consequential question than choosing a single sector to call a bubble.

The News Still Has to Come from Somewhere

A personal editor does not abolish the cost of finding out what happened. Someone visited the courthouse, ran the experiment, interviewed the source, read the filing, or maintained the code. If a system summarizes that work and sends nothing back, the reader may get an excellent morning while the supply of original reporting becomes harder to finance.

Our sources are part of the product for that reason. A reader should be able to reach the original, distinguish a claim from its interpretation, and find the correction. Attribution also makes the system more useful: a model's confident compression is not a substitute for a paper's method or a maintainer's actual warning.

But a link alone does not pay a reporter. An ad-free information system still needs a way to fund information: direct subscriptions, paid feeds, licensing, patronage, public institutions, or some combination. The correct answer will differ across sources. What matters is that the payment buys the work rather than an obligation to endure an attention trap.

Publishers are already exploring a different transaction. Cloudflare introduced pay per crawl in July 2025, allowing participating content owners to set a price for crawler access. That is a proposed payment mechanism, not proof that it has replaced advertising or solved journalism's economics. It does show that access by machines can be treated as a distinct transaction.

Digital abundance should make useful knowledge easier to reach and easier to verify. It should not require pretending that original knowledge appears without labor. Removing the billboard from the reading experience and destroying the institution that paid for the investigation are different achievements. We want the first. We have to build a funding arrangement that does not casually produce the second.

The Next Landlord Is Waiting

The obvious counterattack is to become the agent. If fewer people visit the shop window, own the thing that tells them what to buy. If fewer people read the feed, own the summary. If fewer people open the application, charge for each action the assistant performs inside it. Losing one interface need not mean losing control.

That is the danger in treating an AI-first feed as automatically emancipatory. A system can remove visible ads while selecting answers according to a hidden commercial interest. It can be pleasantly concise and still reserve its best recommendations for the party paying the toll. An advertisement does not become honest because the salesman now speaks in complete paragraphs.

The defensive requirement follows from the structure. The reader must be able to inspect the sources, change the instructions, keep the history, export the result, and replace the model. An application you can modify is useful even when its current model is hosted. It becomes more independent as the expensive dependency can be swapped or run locally.

This is why the open weights and local-inference argument from our first post belongs here. A personal agent controlled by one remote operator can turn the whole web into a dependency on that operator. A personal agent whose useful parts you can retain creates a check on it. The difference is architecture, not the friendliness of the assistant.

No single feature proves independence. A downloadable frontend can still depend on a nonportable account. A local database can still contain a format nobody else understands. The practical question is whether another competent operator could keep the system useful if the original supplier changed the deal. Abundance needs that exit to survive contact with a business model.

The Work Does Not Disappear

There is a less glamorous objection to all this: maintaining software can be harder than making the first version. The person replacing a subscription also inherits updates, security decisions, and the possibility of losing a Saturday to a broken dependency. Sometimes the subscription is cheaper. That is a result an honest ownership argument must permit.

Productivity evidence deserves the same discipline. METR's early-2025 randomized study found experienced contributors took nineteen percent longer with the available AI tools in the studied tasks. Its February 2026 follow-up described serious selection problems in measuring later tools and warned that its new data was an unreliable signal of the current effect. Neither result supplies a universal speed multiplier.

Those limitations do not invalidate a functioning tool. They tell us what to measure: total time, errors, upkeep, and the cost of recovery, compared with the actual alternative. Count the evening spent debugging alongside the hour saved. Count the exit you gained alongside the support contract you lost.

Digital abundance is also not material abundance by announcement. A model can arrange a report; it cannot make the electricity, grow breakfast, or repair a server merely by describing the process. We made that distinction in Software Was Never the Endpoint. Cheaper cognition can help with physical work, but the hardware, energy, care, and coordination remain real.

What Would Make This Argument Wrong

The simplest failure would be that alternatives remain too difficult to operate. If building the narrow replacement saves money on day one but costs more over a year, many buyers will rationally keep renting. If those failures persist as the tools improve, the scope of the abundance claim shrinks. A world with more prototypes is not necessarily a world with more dependable capability.

A second failure would be that the platforms capture the new distribution. The personal agent becomes another mandatory account, paid placement becomes another opaque ranking, and replacing an application merely moves the recurring bill to a model provider. Users might enjoy a cleaner screen while the topology of power gets worse.

A third would be a collapse in the quality of the information being summarized. If removing publisher traffic leaves fewer original sources, the agent can assemble a beautiful report from a deteriorating record. If the reader's own preferences filter out every unwelcome fact, customization becomes a very expensive way to stay wrong.

These are testable directions rather than reasons to stop. Follow actual cancelled purchases and what replaced them. Check whether the replacement is still used after six months. Measure the full running cost. Examine whether the reader can migrate without losing their work. Look for payment reaching the people who produce the underlying information. Check whether corrections survive into tomorrow's edition.

The hypothesis should lose confidence if the exits remain unusable or the new intermediary owns everything. It should gain confidence when useful replacements persist, people can move between operators, and publishers can be paid without selling the reader's attention. A theory of freedom that never checks whether anybody got freer is just another promotional document.

The Abundance We Can Actually Build

This is where the argument parts company with both the apocalypse and the brochure. The benefit is not an infinite supply of generated things nobody asked for. It is the ability to obtain a useful result without surrendering more of your time and autonomy than the result deserves.

A morning report can end. A calendar can show the day without trying to become a workplace religion. A small business can keep its records without buying the organizational ambitions of the company that made the form. A replacement can be good enough for its owner without becoming a platform for everyone.

Build that kind of capability, then make it possible for someone else to keep it. Publish the parts that generalize. Document the limitations. Preserve the export. Pay for the work that remains scarce. Use the strongest available models to improve the tools you can retain, while making the intelligence dependency easier to change.

The software industry will still have businesses. Advertising will still find customers. Some of the largest firms will benefit enormously from the transition. The thing being challenged is their claim on every useful interaction, and the assumption that people have no economical way around it. That claim deserves more scrutiny than the next demonstration of a chatbot writing a poem.

Our morning programme is small. That is what makes it interesting. It does not need permission from a broadcaster, a recommendation algorithm, or a software vendor's roadmap to exist. It needs sources, judgment, equipment, and work. Those are real requirements. They are also requirements people can increasingly assemble for themselves.

The Smoke Is in the Assumption

We thought the AI was the bubble. Some of it may be. But the application already works, and the old businesses are now facing a customer who can ask a different question: what if I build the part I need, have my system read it first, and publish the replacement so the next person can do the same?

That question does not need to destroy a company to change its behavior. It needs to become credible. Every useful exit weakens the price of compulsory dependence. Every maintainable release makes the next exit cheaper.

The fire is not yet an industry-wide revenue collapse. The smoke is a failed renewal, an avoided purchase, an interface the reader no longer has to endure. Some people already see it. The rest may only notice when the customer stops coming through the door.

The age of digital abundance begins when useful capability becomes something people can keep. Build the exit. Publish the plans. Let the service compete for the renewal.
